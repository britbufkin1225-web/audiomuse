# Thrills, Chills, Frissons, and Skin Orgasms

- Source ID: `harrison-loui-frisson-model`
- Class: scholarly work (peer-reviewed review and theoretical model)
- Authors: Luke Harrison, Psyche Loui
- Publication: Frontiers in Psychology 5, 790, 2014
- Identifier: DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00790
- Stable locator: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00790
- Retrieval: full text retrieved

## Supports

- Physiological markers reported in association with musical frisson: increased heart rate, skin
  conductance responses, changes in respiratory depth, piloerection, and non-dermal responses
  including tears and muscle tension.
- Musical events reported as triggers, attributed to the studies the review summarizes: chord
  progressions descending the circle of fifths to the tonic, melodic appoggiaturas, onset of
  unexpected harmonies, and melodic or harmonic sequences; and from later work, peaks in loudness,
  moments of modulation, and melody in the human vocal register.
- Sudden dynamic leaps are described as a major catalyst across nearly all studies reviewed.
- Familiarity matters: people are reported as more likely to react physically to familiar music.
- The terminology problem: the review states that chills lacks operative consensus, that authors
  disagree on whether piloerection is definitional, and it advocates frisson as the clearer term.
- The causal-direction caution AudioMuse needs: the review notes that finding more frisson during sad
  music does not rule out the alternative that musical attributes of sad music elicit both the frisson
  and the sadness concurrently.

## Does not settle

- Prevalence. The review reports modest and inconsistent figures from individual studies and offers
  no single rate.
- Generality across genres and cultures. The authors state that studies overwhelmingly use Western
  classical music and college-student samples, which limits ecological validity.
- Whether any listed musical feature causes frisson. The relationships reported are correlational and
  the review says the direction of causality has generally been assumed rather than tested.
