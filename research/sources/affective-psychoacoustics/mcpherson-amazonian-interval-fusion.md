# Perceptual Fusion of Musical Notes by Native Amazonians

- Source ID: `mcpherson-amazonian-interval-fusion`
- Class: scholarly work (peer-reviewed primary research)
- Authors: Malinda J. McPherson, Sophia E. Dolan, Alex Durango, Tomas Ossandon, Joaquin Valdes,
  Eduardo A. Undurraga, Nori Jacoby, Ricardo A. Godoy, Josh H. McDermott
- Publication: Nature Communications 11, 2786, 2020
- Identifier: DOI 10.1038/s41467-020-16448-6; PMCID PMC7270137
- Stable locator: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7270137/
- Retrieval: full text retrieved

## Supports

- Both Western (Boston) and native Amazonian (Tsimane) listeners fused simple-integer-ratio intervals
  more readily than dissonant ones, with the octave producing the strongest fusion in both groups.
- Fusion did not predict pleasantness. In Westerners the octave was the most fused interval but not
  the most pleasant, and individual differences in fusion showed no correlation with consonance
  preference.
- The Amazonian listeners showed no preference for consonant over dissonant intervals while showing
  the same fusion pattern as Westerners.
- The authors' conclusion: universal perceptual biases exist and could partially constrain musical
  systems, but they shape aesthetic responses only indirectly, and those responses are instead
  determined by what is prevalent in the musical system an individual experiences.

## Does not settle

- Whether roughness, harmonicity, or familiarity is the dominant contributor to Western consonance
  preference. The paper separates fusion from preference; it does not rank the remaining candidates.
- Anything about chords in musical context. The stimuli were isolated two-note intervals.
- Any claim that consonance preference is purely learned or purely innate. The finding is that
  perception and aesthetics dissociate, which is a narrower statement than either position.
- Anything about emotion. Pleasantness rating is not an emotion measure.
