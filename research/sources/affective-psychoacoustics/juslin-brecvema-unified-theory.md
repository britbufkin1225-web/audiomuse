# From Everyday Emotions to Aesthetic Emotions: Towards a Unified Theory of Musical Emotions

- Source ID: `juslin-brecvema-unified-theory`
- Class: scholarly work (peer-reviewed review article, published with open peer commentary)
- Author: Patrik N. Juslin
- Publication: Physics of Life Reviews 10 (3), 235–266, 2013
- Identifier: DOI 10.1016/j.plrev.2013.05.008
- Stable locator: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.plrev.2013.05.008
- Retrieval: full text retrieved

## Supports

- The BRECVEMA framework and its eight named mechanisms: brain stem reflex, rhythmic entrainment,
  evaluative conditioning, contagion, visual imagery, episodic memory, musical expectancy, and
  aesthetic judgment. The earlier six-mechanism version was BRECVEM; rhythmic entrainment was added in
  response to commentary on that version, and aesthetic judgment is the addition this article
  proposes.
- Brain stem reflex is described as a response to sounds that are sudden, loud, or dissonant, or that
  feature accelerating patterns, and is characterized as quick, automatic, and unlearned.
- Rhythmic entrainment is described as an external musical rhythm drawing an internal bodily rhythm
  toward a common periodicity, with the adjusted rhythm then spreading to felt emotion through
  proprioceptive feedback.
- Musical expectancy is described as confirmation, delay, or violation of expectations formed from a
  listener's previous experience of the same musical style, with Meyer credited for the argument.
- The framework's central constraint: mechanisms respond to a musical event comprising music,
  listener, and context together. The music affords a response but does not guarantee it.
- The article's working definitions separate affect, emotion, mood, feeling, arousal, preference, and
  aesthetic judgment, and separate perceiving an emotion in music from feeling one in response to it.
- Music is reported to arouse emotion in roughly 55–65% of listening episodes on average, with wide
  individual differences in prevalence.

## Does not settle

- Whether the framework is correct. It is presented as theory, published with peer commentary, and
  argues its case rather than reporting a decisive test.
- The neural implementation of any mechanism. Juslin states that a description of a psychological
  process should not be confused with its neural implementation or with the experience it explains.
- Any mapping from a musical feature to a specific emotion. The framework describes the process by
  which sound acquires meaning, not a feature-to-emotion table.
