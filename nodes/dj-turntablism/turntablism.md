# Turntablism

Session 3 records the conceptual turn plainly: the turntable stops being merely a playback device and becomes an instrument. The claim AudioMuse keeps from that section is that recorded music can be performed as raw material. A finished record ceases to be a fixed object and becomes something a performer can enter, stop, reverse, repeat, and re-order.

The sessions name a family of techniques that develop around this idea — cueing, backspinning, cutting, scratching, and beat juggling — and they develop breakbeat DJing in detail: two copies of one record alternated so that a short rhythmic break can be extended indefinitely. That is a rhythmic act, which is why AudioMuse records turntablism as influencing `rhythm`: the performer produces a rhythmic result that does not exist in either source recording on its own.

The same practice connects to `sampling` as a musical activity. Both can isolate, repeat, and rearrange recorded fragments, and the `sampling` node's own open question — how to separate signal sampling from the musical reuse of recordings — is answered on this side of the graph. Turntablism is one contributing practice among several rather than the sole cause, which is why the stored edge is `contributes_to`.

Of the technique names above, only scratching and break extension are developed in the registered sessions. Backspinning, cutting, cueing, and beat juggling appear as listed terms, so AudioMuse records them here as named techniques awaiting sourced description rather than defining them from outside material.
