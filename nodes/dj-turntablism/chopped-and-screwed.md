# Chopped and Screwed

The practice is two operations, and defining it as "music played slower" drops half of it. The University of Houston's curator states both parts together: Screw slowed down all of the music and repeated certain beats, words, and phrases. Reduction of playback rate is the continuous operation; chopping is the discrete one; the result is a version of a record that does not exist in the source at any speed.

```text
existing record
  -> sustained rate reduction  (continuous: pitch, tempo, and duration move together)
  -> manual repetition          (discrete: phrases, words, and beats re-struck)
  -> freestyle material added over the treated source
  -> recorded in one pass to tape
```

`slowed-playback`, `chopping`, and `repetition` hold the three operations and their technical consequences; this node holds the practice that combines them. The combination is what makes it composition rather than processing: rate reduction alone yields a slower record, and repetition alone yields an edit, while together they redistribute emphasis, stretch the space between transients, lower the vocal register, and re-order what the listener is asked to attend to.

Two further properties are historically specific and easy to lose. It was performed, not edited: the chops were made by hand on the running record with a second copy available, and the pass that was recorded is the take that exists. And it was applied to whole mixes rather than to isolated tracks, so the treatment is a property of the tape as a listening object rather than of any single song.

The `influences` edge into `chopped-and-slowed` marks the later digital descendant without asserting that every slowed track descends from this practice. That distinction is developed on the descendant node, where it belongs.

What this node does not state: a canonical percentage or playback speed. No retrieved source establishes one, published figures vary, and asserting a universal Screw speed would invent precision the evidence does not carry.
