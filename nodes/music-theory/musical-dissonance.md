# Musical Dissonance

Dissonance in AudioMuse is a two-layer concept, and most confusion about it comes from collapsing the layers.

The lower layer is peripheral. When components fall close enough together to interact within a critical band, the result is the sensation `roughness` describes and `sensory-dissonance` scopes. It is measurable, it depends on the actual spectrum rather than on the notated interval, and it changes with register, voicing, and timbre while the score stays the same. Distortion is the extreme case: it generates additional partials, so a chord that was clean becomes a much denser field of close-spaced components, and the peripheral dissonance rises without a single note changing.

The upper layer is tonal and learned. A tritone in a functional progression is unstable because a listener enculturated to that system expects a particular continuation. That instability is a relation between an event and a context; it is not a property the interval carries around with it. Session 2's framing of composition as expectation management is precisely this layer, and `musical-expectation` holds it.

The two layers can move in opposite directions, which is why keeping them separate is practical rather than pedantic. A close cluster played on soft flutes in a high register has high tonal dissonance and modest peripheral interaction. A perfect fifth on two heavily overdriven guitars has no tonal dissonance and a great deal of peripheral interaction. Any account with one dial cannot describe both.

What AudioMuse will not say is that dissonance is negative. McPherson and colleagues found listeners who showed the Western fusion pattern and no preference for consonance over dissonance at all, and the HEAD acoustics note is explicit that roughness is judged annoying only when the sound is unwanted. Entire genres are built on sustained dissonance and are enjoyed; the response depends on the material, the context, and the listener, exactly as `affective-psychoacoustics` requires.
