# Auditory Looming

Approaching and receding sounds are not treated equally by the auditory system, and the asymmetry is early enough to be interesting.

Ignatiadis, Baier, Toth, and Baumgartner describe the auditory looming bias as an early perceptual phenomenon reflecting higher alertness to approaching auditory objects than to receding ones. In their study it appeared behaviourally as higher accuracy and faster reaction times for looming than for receding trials, and they describe the evaluation as fast and probably automatic.

The methodological detail that makes this citable is what they varied. Earlier demonstrations typically used rising intensity, which leaves open the objection that the effect is a loudness effect. These authors held overall intensity constant and produced the looming percept by manipulating spatial spectral cues instead. The bias survived. So it is not simply that louder gets attention.

What AudioMuse will not do with this is convert it into an emotion. Alertness and reaction time are not affective measures, and the paper contains no claim about threat, fear, or tension. The authors also record real reservations about their connectivity analysis, note that source localization used a template brain rather than individual anatomy, and describe an unresolved gap between their single-trial and averaged results.

Where it belongs in Phase 12E is as a candidate mechanism, sitting close to what Juslin calls brain stem reflex — a fast, unlearned response to acoustic properties signalling that something needs attention. Risers, filter sweeps, reverb pulled in toward a dry close position, and swelling sub-bass are all, structurally, approach cues, and this node records that the underlying perceptual asymmetry is real without asserting that the musical gesture inherits it. Testing that inheritance is exactly what experiment `apparent-space-and-affect` proposes.
