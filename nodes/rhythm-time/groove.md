# Groove

Groove is one of the few places in this literature where a musical intuition has been measured cleanly enough to state.

Witek, Clarke, Wallentin, Kringelbach, and Vuust asked listeners to rate drum patterns of varying syncopation for pleasure and for wanting to move. The relationship was an inverted U: medium degrees of syncopation elicited the most desire to move and the most pleasure. Low-syncopation patterns rated higher for movement than high-syncopation patterns, but both sat below the medium condition. Neither metronomic regularity nor maximal complexity wins.

Their proposed explanation is the one that connects this node to the rest of Phase 12E. Medium syncopation, they suggest, balances enough rhythmic predictability for metre to be perceived against enough complexity for those expectations to be violated. High syncopation may prevent successful entrainment altogether and so inhibit the desire to move. Groove, on this account, is not a property of a pattern but a relationship between a pattern and a listener's capacity to predict it — which is `musical-expectation` and `rhythmic-entrainment` acting together on the same material.

Two honest limits. What was measured was ratings, and the authors state that the relationship between those ratings and overt body movement remains unknown. And the shape was established for the patterns tested, not for every genre, tempo, or listener population; the paper does not license an optimum that transfers.

Grahn and Brett's finding that beat-inducing rhythms engage the basal ganglia and supplementary motor area supplies the motor half from a different direction, and their observation that perceptual accents can arise from temporal structure alone matters here specifically: a chop that leaves a gap creates an accent whether or not a drum was struck. `temporal-displacement` holds the microtiming side of the same question, and Phase 12E treats it as open whether the temporal-DSP operations sit on Witek's curve or run off it.
