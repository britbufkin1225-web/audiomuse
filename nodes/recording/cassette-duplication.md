# Cassette Duplication

Duplication is the step that turns a recording into a distribution system. A cassette master exists once; copies of it can be made anywhere there are two decks, without a pressing plant, a distributor, or a minimum order. That property is what allowed a tape economy to operate entirely outside the industrial chain described on `music-distribution`, selling directly to buyers who wanted a specific tape.

The technical cost is generational loss. Each analog copy adds noise and loses high-frequency detail, and copies made from copies compound it, so two people holding the same title may not be holding the same audio. Duplication speed matters as well: real-time dubbing plays the master at normal speed, while high-speed dubbing runs both transports faster and trades fidelity for throughput. When an unauthorized copy circulates, it is usually further from the master than the seller's own stock, which means informal duplication degrades the material as it spreads it.

Labeling belongs to the same layer. A handwritten title, a catalog number, or a printed insert is how a copy is identified afterwards, and a catalog kept as a list of numbered titles — as at the Houston shop where titles were ordered by name or number from a whiteboard — is a distribution index in physical form.

Preservation is the long tail of the same facts. Magnetic tape is a decaying carrier played on increasingly rare machines, so a scene documented mainly on cassette is a scene whose primary evidence has a shelf life. That is one reason archival holdings of tapes and equipment, not only of recordings, are recorded in `music-archiving`.

The University of Houston finding aid describes Screw's master-production chain, including a master cassette and further four-track slowing, but it does not describe how sales copies were duplicated. This node therefore states no Screw-specific duplication deck, dubbing speed, tape stock, or copy-bank workflow; the general mechanics stand and the specific sales-copy case remains open.
