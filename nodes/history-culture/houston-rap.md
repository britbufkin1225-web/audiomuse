# Houston Rap

Houston rap is recorded here as an economy first and a style second. The city's rap infrastructure was built by people who owned the labels, pressed and duplicated the product, and sold it locally, and the historically durable claim is structural: national recognition arrived after the local system already worked, not before.

Two strands run through the 1980s and 1990s and should not be merged. One is label-based and eventually national — Rap-A-Lot, the Geto Boys, and the studio and engineering practice around them. The other is collective and deliberately independent — the South Park Coalition, and later the tape economy around DJ Screw, which sold directly to listeners without radio or a distributor in the loop. Accounts of the South Park Coalition stress that it operated without radio airplay or major-label money; accounts of Screw describe sales from a house on Greenstone Street. These are different circulation models inside one city, and the `music-distribution` node holds what they have in common.

The `enables` edge into `chopped-and-screwed` states the dependency in the direction the evidence supports. The technique did not arrive from outside and get adopted; it developed inside a scene that already had rappers willing to freestyle at three in the morning, a listener base that would buy a cassette the same week, and no gatekeeper whose format expectations had to be satisfied. The practice was possible because the economy was local.

`houston-rap --contributes_to--> third-coast` is deliberately weaker. Houston is one of several scenes that the regional term gathers, and the term's boundaries are contested, so the edge records participation rather than ownership.

What this node does not claim: any date for a first Houston rap record, any ranking of who came first, and any single explanation of the scene's sound. The chronology document holds the sourced sequence, including the points where sources disagree.
