# Music Distribution

AudioMuse models how music reaches a listener because the answer changes what gets made. The general chain runs:

```text
ARTIST -> LABEL -> PHYSICAL RELEASE -> DISTRIBUTOR -> RECORD STORE -> LISTENER
```

Houston supplies unusually clear evidence for each link, and also for what happens when a link is skipped. Duke and Peacock show the earliest form: a Houston owner with a catalog, a studio relationship, and a national reach for blues, R&B, and gospel from the late 1940s. Southwest Wholesale shows the wholesaling link operating at scale for independent rap for twenty-seven years until 2003. Pen & Pixel shows the packaging link, where a cover was doing distribution work by making a record recognizable in a rack. Screw tapes show the chain being bypassed entirely: sale directly from a house and then a shop, with catalog numbers but no distributor, followed by unauthorized duplication that moved the music into places the maker had no arrangement with.

Bootlegging belongs in this node rather than in a moral aside. An informal copy is a distribution mechanism with its own reach, its own quality loss, and its own effect on the original seller, and it is part of how this music circulated whether or not anyone approved.

The single outbound edge, `music-distribution --influences--> houston-rap`, records the direction that the evidence supports: how the material could be sold materially shaped the scene. The reverse claim — that the scene reshaped distribution — is plausible but is not established by anything retrieved here, so it is not stored.
