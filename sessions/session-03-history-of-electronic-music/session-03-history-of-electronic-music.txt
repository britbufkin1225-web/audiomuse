# AudioMuse — History of Electronic Music Timeline

Electronic music did not begin with synthesizers, clubs, or computers. Its history starts with experiments in **electricity, acoustics, recording, signal generation, and new ways of controlling sound**. Over more than a century, those experiments evolved into synthesizers, sampling, turntablism, house, techno, hip-hop, ambient, drum and bass, EDM, laptop production, spatial audio, and AI-assisted music systems.

---

## 1870s–1890s — Electricity Meets Sound

### 1876 — Telephone technology advances electrical audio transmission
Alexander Graham Bell's telephone work demonstrates that sound can be converted into an electrical signal, transmitted, and reconstructed.

**Why it matters:**
Electronic music ultimately depends on this basic concept:

**acoustic energy → electrical signal → processing → sound**

This same chain eventually becomes the foundation of microphones, mixers, amplifiers, synthesizers, recording systems, and audio interfaces.

---

### 1877 — Thomas Edison demonstrates the phonograph
The phonograph makes recorded sound reproducible.

Before recording, music existed primarily as a live event or written notation.

Now sound itself could become an **object**.

**Electronic-music connection:**
Recording eventually makes possible:

- looping
- sampling
- scratching
- remixing
- tape manipulation
- DJ culture
- digital audio

---

### 1897 — The Telharmonium is patented
Thaddeus Cahill develops the **Telharmonium**, an enormous electromechanical instrument using rotating tone wheels to generate electrical signals.

It is one of the earliest significant electronic/electromechanical musical instruments.

**Conceptual importance:**
Instead of vibrating strings or air columns directly, musical tones can be **generated electrically**.

---

# 1900s–1920s — The First Electronic Instruments

## 1906 — The Audion vacuum tube
Lee de Forest develops the Audion triode vacuum tube.

Vacuum tubes enable:

- amplification
- oscillation
- radio
- electronic signal processing

They become fundamental to early electronic instruments and audio equipment.

---

## 1910s–1920s — Electronic oscillators become musical tools
Engineers discover increasingly practical ways to create continuous electronic oscillations.

These circuits eventually become the ancestors of synthesizer oscillators.

---

## 1920 — The Theremin

Russian inventor **Leon Theremin** develops the Theremin.

The performer controls it without touching the instrument.

Two antennas control approximately:

- pitch
- amplitude

Movement of the performer's hands changes electromagnetic fields.

### Why the Theremin matters

It demonstrates three revolutionary ideas:

1. Music can be generated electronically.
2. An instrument does not require traditional mechanical keys or strings.
3. Human gestures can control electronic sound.

That third idea connects surprisingly well to modern technologies including:

- motion controllers
- webcams
- hand tracking
- MIDI gestural controllers
- VR instruments

---

## 1928 — Ondes Martenot

Maurice Martenot introduces the **Ondes Martenot**.

It produces electronic tones controlled through a keyboard and movable ring interface.

It becomes one of the earliest electronic instruments to gain serious acceptance in orchestral composition.

---

# 1930s — Electronic Instruments Become More Musical

## 1930 — Trautonium

Friedrich Trautwein develops the **Trautonium**.

Instead of a conventional keyboard, the player presses a wire against a rail to control pitch.

Later versions contribute significantly to early electronic sound design.

---

## 1935 — Hammond Organ

Laurens Hammond introduces the **Hammond organ**.

Although electromechanical rather than a modern electronic synthesizer, it uses rotating tone wheels and electrical amplification.

It becomes enormously influential in:

- jazz
- gospel
- rock
- soul
- funk

The Hammond shows that electrically generated musical instruments can become mainstream rather than laboratory curiosities.

---

## Late 1930s — Magnetic tape technology develops

Magnetic tape recording improves significantly in Germany.

Tape becomes one of the most important technologies in electronic-music history.

Why?

Because musicians can physically manipulate recorded sound.

---

# 1940s — Recorded Sound Becomes Compositional Material

## 1948 — Musique concrète

French composer and engineer **Pierre Schaeffer** develops what becomes known as **musique concrète**.

Instead of composing primarily with musical notation, Schaeffer works with **recorded sounds**.

Sources include:

- trains
- machinery
- voices
- percussion
- environmental noise

Recordings are manipulated through:

- looping
- changing playback speed
- reversing
- editing
- layering

This is one of the direct ancestors of **sampling**.

### Major conceptual shift

Before:

> Instrument → note → composition

Now:

> Recorded sound → transformation → composition

Anything audible could become music.

---

## 1948 — Étude aux chemins de fer

Schaeffer creates *Étude aux chemins de fer* using recordings of trains.

It becomes an early landmark work of musique concrète.

---

# 1950s — Tape Music and Electronic Studios

## Early 1950s — Elektronische Musik in Germany

Composers associated with Cologne begin producing **Elektronische Musik** using electronically generated tones rather than primarily recorded environmental sounds.

Important figures include:

- Karlheinz Stockhausen
- Herbert Eimert

A philosophical division appears:

### Musique concrète

Starts with **recorded real-world sounds**.

### Elektronische Musik

Starts with **electronically synthesized tones**.

Modern production merges both approaches constantly.

---

## 1951 — Studio for Electronic Music in Cologne

The West German Broadcasting studio becomes a major center for electronic composition.

Musicians work with:

- oscillators
- filters
- tape machines
- noise generators
- ring modulation

The electronic studio begins functioning almost like a giant analog synthesizer assembled from individual pieces of laboratory equipment.

---

## 1952 — John Cage's *Williams Mix*

John Cage produces one of the early major tape compositions using precisely edited magnetic tape.

Hundreds of tape fragments are arranged according to compositional rules.

---

## 1954 — Stockhausen's *Studie II*

Stockhausen creates *Studie II*, one of the landmark compositions made primarily from electronically generated sounds.

---

## 1957 — RCA Mark II Sound Synthesizer

The **RCA Mark II** is installed at the Columbia-Princeton Electronic Music Center.

It is an enormous programmable electronic music system.

Users can control parameters such as:

- pitch
- amplitude
- envelope
- timbre

It foreshadows later sequencers and synthesizers.

---

# 1960s — The Synthesizer Era Begins

## Early 1960s — Modular synthesis develops

Engineers begin developing instruments combining reusable electronic modules such as:

- oscillators
- filters
- amplifiers
- envelope generators
- sequencers

Instead of building an entirely new circuit for every composition, musicians can **patch modules together**.

---

## 1963–1964 — Don Buchla develops modular systems

Engineer **Don Buchla** develops electronic instruments on the U.S. West Coast.

Buchla systems emphasize experimental control interfaces and sequencing rather than traditional keyboard performance.

This later contributes to the idea of **West Coast synthesis**.

---

## 1964 — Robert Moog demonstrates modular synthesizer technology

Robert Moog develops voltage-controlled synthesizer modules.

Major concepts include:

- VCO — voltage-controlled oscillator
- VCF — voltage-controlled filter
- VCA — voltage-controlled amplifier

These remain fundamental synthesizer concepts today.

---

## 1966 — Buchla 100 series

The Buchla 100 becomes one of the earliest commercially available modular synthesizer systems.

---

## 1967 — Moog modular synthesizers gain attention

Moog synthesizers become increasingly visible to composers and recording studios.

The synthesizer is moving from academic laboratories toward popular music production.

---

## 1968 — *Switched-On Bach*

Wendy Carlos releases **Switched-On Bach**, performing Bach compositions using a Moog synthesizer.

The album becomes commercially successful.

### Why it matters

It proves synthesizers can produce sophisticated, commercially viable music rather than merely experimental sound effects.

Synthesizers suddenly attract much wider attention.

---

# 1970s — Synthesizers Enter Popular Music

## 1970 — Minimoog Model D

Moog introduces the **Minimoog Model D**.

Unlike enormous modular systems, it is:

- portable
- integrated
- performance-oriented
- relatively simple to operate

It becomes one of history's most influential synthesizers.

Especially important for:

- bass
- leads
- funk
- progressive rock
- disco
- electronic music

---

## Early 1970s — Sequencers expand

Analog step sequencers allow musicians to automate repetitive note patterns.

This helps establish one of electronic music's defining characteristics:

> machine-controlled repetition

Sequencers become central to:

- techno
- house
- trance
- synth-pop
- electro

---

## 1974 — Kraftwerk releases *Autobahn*

German group **Kraftwerk** brings a highly electronic aesthetic into popular music.

Their work combines:

- synthesizers
- drum machines
- vocoders
- repetitive rhythms
- minimalist melodies
- technology-centered imagery

Kraftwerk becomes profoundly influential on:

- hip-hop
- electro
- techno
- synth-pop
- industrial
- dance music

---

## Mid-1970s — Disco production expands electronic techniques

Disco producers increasingly use:

- synthesizers
- electronic keyboards
- studio effects
- extended mixes
- drum-machine-like repetition

Dance-floor production begins developing its own technological language.

---

## 1977 — Giorgio Moroder and Donna Summer — *I Feel Love*

Donna Summer's **I Feel Love**, produced by Giorgio Moroder and Pete Bellotte, becomes a landmark electronic dance recording.

Its rhythmic foundation is largely synthesized.

The track demonstrates how electronic sequencing can drive an entire dance record.

Its influence on later:

- house
- techno
- synth-pop
- trance
- electronic dance music

is enormous.

---

## 1977 — Roland releases the MC-8 Microcomposer

One of the earliest commercially available digital sequencers.

Music sequencing begins transitioning from analog voltage patterns toward digital control.

---

# Late 1970s–Early 1980s — Hip-Hop Changes the Meaning of Recorded Music

## 1970s — DJ culture grows in the Bronx

DJs including **DJ Kool Herc**, **Grandmaster Flash**, and others develop techniques centered on records.

DJs isolate and extend rhythmic **breaks**.

Two copies of the same record can be alternated to extend a drum break.

This becomes **breakbeat DJing**.

---

## Turntablism emerges

The turntable stops being merely a playback device.

It becomes an instrument.

Techniques develop including:

- cueing
- backspinning
- cutting
- scratching
- beat juggling

This is one of the most important conceptual developments in electronic music:

> Recorded music can be performed as raw material.

---

## 1975–1977 — Grand Wizzard Theodore develops scratching

Grand Wizzard Theodore is widely credited with developing or popularizing the scratch technique during the late 1970s.

Scratching transforms:

**recorded waveform → hand-controlled musical gesture**

---

# 1980s — Drum Machines, MIDI, Sampling and Digital Synthesis

## 1980 — Roland TR-808

Roland releases the **TR-808 Rhythm Composer**.

Initially it is not a major commercial success.

Later its distinctive synthetic drums become foundational to:

- hip-hop
- electro
- Miami bass
- techno
- house
- trap
- pop

Famous sounds include:

- huge 808 kick
- handclap
- cowbell
- snare
- hi-hats

The 808 becomes arguably one of the most culturally influential electronic instruments ever made.

---

## 1981 — Roland TB-303

Roland releases the **TB-303 Bass Line**.

It was intended to simulate a bass guitarist for practice.

It does not do that particularly convincingly.

Which, naturally, makes it perfect for inventing an entirely new genre.

Its resonant filter and sequencing behavior later become central to **acid house**.

---

## 1982 — Roland TR-606

The TR-606 drum machine is introduced as a companion to the TB-303.

It later becomes valued in electronic and experimental production.

---

## 1982 — Fairlight CMI grows in influence

The **Fairlight CMI** becomes one of the most important early commercial digital sampling systems.

Musicians can record sound digitally and play it chromatically from a keyboard.

Sampling moves from experimental tape techniques into an integrated digital instrument.

---

## 1982–1983 — MIDI is standardized

**MIDI — Musical Instrument Digital Interface** becomes a standard communication protocol.

For the first time, equipment from different manufacturers can communicate using standardized musical messages.

MIDI can transmit information such as:

- note on
- note off
- velocity
- pitch bend
- control changes
- clock

### Why MIDI matters

It allows:

```text id="kn80uo"
Keyboard
    ↓
Synthesizer
    ↓
Drum machine
    ↓
Sequencer
```

to function together as an electronic system.

MIDI becomes one of the fundamental protocols of digital music.

---

## 1983 — Yamaha DX7

Yamaha releases the **DX7**.

It uses **FM synthesis** rather than traditional analog subtractive synthesis.

Its distinctive digital sounds dominate portions of 1980s pop.

Famous DX7-style sounds include:

- electric piano
- bells
- metallic percussion
- bright basses

Digital synthesis becomes mainstream.

---

## 1983 — Roland TR-909

The **TR-909** combines analog drum synthesis with digital samples.

Its sounds become foundational to:

- house
- techno
- acid house
- trance

The 909 kick and hi-hats remain electronic-dance standards decades later.

---

# 1980s — House Music

## Early–mid 1980s — Chicago house develops

DJs and producers in Chicago develop what becomes known as **house music**.

Important figures include:

- Frankie Knuckles
- Jesse Saunders
- Marshall Jefferson
- Ron Hardy

House combines influences from:

- disco
- soul
- funk
- electronic music
- drum machines
- synthesizers

A typical structure includes:

**four-on-the-floor kick + repetitive groove + bass + samples/vocals**

---

## 1984 — "On and On"

Jesse Saunders' **On and On** is often cited among the earliest commercially released Chicago house records.

---

# 1980s — Detroit Techno

## Mid-1980s — Detroit techno develops

Detroit artists including:

- Juan Atkins
- Derrick May
- Kevin Saunderson

develop a futuristic electronic style influenced by:

- Kraftwerk
- funk
- electro
- science fiction
- industrial Detroit

These artists become known collectively as the **Belleville Three**.

Techno emphasizes:

- machines
- sequencers
- synthetic rhythm
- repetition
- futurism

---

# Mid–Late 1980s — Acid House

Producers discover that manipulating the TB-303's:

- cutoff
- resonance
- envelope
- accent

while patterns play produces an unusual squelching sound.

The failure of the 303 as a realistic bass replacement becomes one of electronic music's greatest happy accidents.

---

## 1987 — Acid house explodes

Tracks such as Phuture's **Acid Tracks** help define the genre.

The TB-303 becomes legendary.

---

# 1980s — Sampling Transforms Hip-Hop

Affordable digital samplers increasingly enter studios.

Important machines include:

- E-mu SP-12
- E-mu SP-1200
- Akai MPC series

Producers can:

1. sample records,
2. chop sounds,
3. rearrange them,
4. pitch them,
5. sequence them.

The **sample producer** becomes a new kind of composer.

---

# 1988 — Akai MPC60

Designed in collaboration with Roger Linn, the **MPC60** combines:

- sampling
- drum pads
- sequencing

The MPC becomes enormously influential in:

- hip-hop
- R&B
- electronic music

Finger drumming develops into a distinct performance technique.

---

# Late 1980s–1990s — Rave Culture

Electronic dance scenes expand dramatically in Europe.

Warehouse parties and raves become associated with styles including:

- acid house
- techno
- breakbeat
- hardcore
- trance

The DJ becomes increasingly central as both performer and curator.

---

# 1990s — Electronic Music Fragments Into Many Genres

## Techno expands

Regional styles develop throughout:

- Detroit
- Berlin
- Birmingham
- Belgium
- elsewhere in Europe

---

## House branches into multiple forms

Examples include:

- deep house
- garage house
- progressive house
- tribal house
- French house

---

## Early 1990s — Trance

Trance develops around:

- repetitive melodic sequences
- arpeggios
- synthesizer pads
- long builds
- euphoric harmonic structures

---

## Breakbeat culture expands

Producers increasingly sample and rearrange drum breaks.

Important break sources include the famous **Amen Break**.

This helps lead toward:

- jungle
- drum and bass
- breakcore

---

# 1992–1994 — Jungle

UK producers develop **jungle**, characterized by:

- heavily chopped breakbeats
- reggae/dub influence
- deep bass
- fast tempos
- extensive sampling

Digital sampling becomes almost a form of microscopic rhythmic composition.

---

# Mid-1990s — Drum and Bass

Jungle evolves into and overlaps with **drum and bass**.

Production emphasizes:

- complex break manipulation
- sub-bass
- synthesis
- sampling
- approximately 160–180 BPM rhythms

---

# 1990s — Intelligent Dance Music / IDM

Artists explore electronic music intended for both listening and dancing.

Important artists include:

- Aphex Twin
- Autechre
- Boards of Canada

Genres become increasingly experimental with:

- unusual rhythms
- digital processing
- algorithmic composition
- complex synthesis

---

# 1990s — The Digital Audio Workstation Emerges

Computers become powerful enough for serious audio production.

Software platforms increasingly combine:

- recording
- MIDI
- sequencing
- editing
- mixing
- effects

Music production starts moving from specialized hardware studios into computers.

---

# 1996 — Steinberg introduces VST

**Virtual Studio Technology** allows third-party software effects and later instruments to operate inside compatible DAWs.

This transforms music production.

Hardware devices can increasingly be represented in software.

---

# Late 1990s — Software Synthesizers

Computer CPUs become capable of generating increasingly sophisticated synthesis in real time.

Production gradually shifts from:

```text id="xaukuo"
Room full of hardware
```

toward:

```text id="84olr9"
Computer + audio interface + controller
```

---

# 1998 — Propellerhead ReBirth

**ReBirth RB-338** emulates instruments inspired by:

- TB-303
- TR-808
- TR-909

It demonstrates that classic electronic hardware workflows can be convincingly reproduced in software.

---

# 1999 — Ableton founded

Ableton begins developing software focused on electronic composition and live performance.

---

# 2000s — The Laptop Becomes a Studio

## 2001 — Ableton Live

**Ableton Live** is released.

Its Session View introduces a highly flexible method for triggering:

- loops
- clips
- samples
- sequences

Live becomes particularly influential in electronic music because the DAW itself becomes a **performance instrument**.

---

## 2000s — Home production explodes

Affordable:

- computers
- audio interfaces
- software synthesizers
- microphones
- MIDI controllers

dramatically lower the barrier to music production.

A professional-style electronic studio can increasingly fit on a desk.

---

# 2000s — Digital DJing

DJ software begins replacing some traditional vinyl workflows.

Systems develop using:

- digital music libraries
- controllers
- CDJs
- timecode vinyl

---

## Digital Vinyl Systems — DVS

Systems such as Serato Scratch Live allow DJs to manipulate digital files using physical turntables and timecode records.

This elegantly combines:

**vinyl mechanics + digital audio**

Traditional scratching survives into the digital era without abandoning the turntable interface.

---

# 2000s — Dubstep

Dubstep develops in the UK.

Characteristics include:

- heavy sub-bass
- sparse percussion
- syncopation
- dub influence

Later versions emphasize aggressive modulated bass design.

---

# Late 2000s–2010s — EDM Becomes Massive

Electronic dance music enters large-scale commercial pop culture.

Festival scenes expand dramatically.

Genres include:

- electro house
- progressive house
- dubstep
- big-room house
- trap
- future bass

Electronic production becomes deeply integrated into mainstream pop.

---

# 2010s — Controllers and Hybrid Performance

DJ controllers increasingly combine:

- jog wheels
- performance pads
- mixers
- effects
- looping
- cue control

Software ecosystems such as:

- Serato
- Traktor
- Rekordbox
- VirtualDJ

make advanced DJ workflows accessible without large hardware studios.

---

# 2010s — Stems and Source Separation

Researchers improve algorithms for separating mixed recordings into components such as:

- vocals
- drums
- bass
- instruments

This begins changing:

- remixing
- DJing
- sampling
- restoration
- karaoke
- archival work

Eventually DJs can isolate stems in real time.

---

# 2010s — Modular Synthesis Returns

Interest in analog and modular synthesis resurges.

The **Eurorack** modular format becomes especially popular.

Ironically, after decades of increasingly powerful computers, musicians rediscover the appeal of physically patching cables between oscillators and filters.

Apparently humanity cannot resist making expensive spaghetti out of patch cables.

---

# 2010s — Hardware Revival

New interest develops around:

- analog synthesizers
- drum machines
- grooveboxes
- cassette recording
- vinyl
- modular equipment

Electronic music becomes increasingly **hybrid**:

```text id="7acuuz"
Analog hardware
+
Digital software
+
Sampling
+
Controllers
+

```

# 2020s — AI and Machine Learning Enter Audio Production

Machine-learning systems increasingly perform tasks including:

- source separation
- denoising
- transcription
- mastering assistance
- instrument identification
- audio restoration
- voice transformation
- sound generation
- music generation

The computer begins analyzing and generating audio not merely as waveform data but increasingly as **semantic musical information**.

---

# 2020s — Stem-Based DJing

Modern DJ platforms increasingly offer real-time stem separation.

A DJ can isolate components such as:

```text id="8ddsh9"
Vocals
Drums
Bass
Instrumental
```

from a mixed recording.

This turns a finished track into something closer to a manipulable multitrack session.

For turntablism, this is particularly significant.

A DJ can potentially:

- scratch only the vocal,
- remove drums from one track,
- layer another rhythm,
- isolate bass,
- create live mashups.

The border between **DJ and producer** becomes increasingly thin.

---

# 2020s — Spatial Audio

Consumer systems increasingly support immersive formats and binaural rendering.

Music production expands beyond traditional:

**left ↔ right stereo**

toward:

```text id="isuizf"
             Above
               ↑

Left ← Listener → Right

               ↓
             Below

       Front ↔ Rear
```

This introduces composition in three-dimensional acoustic space.

---

# 2020s — Generative and AI Music Systems

Generative systems increasingly produce or manipulate:

- songs
- stems
- voices
- instrument parts
- sound effects
- ambient environments

This creates new questions involving:

- authorship
- copyright
- training data
- attribution
- originality
- performance
- human-machine collaboration

These issues will likely become as important to electronic music as sampling law became during the rise of hip-hop.

# Five Revolutions That Define Electronic Music

## Revolution 1 — Sound became recordable

The phonograph and magnetic tape transformed sound into something that could be stored and reused.

---

## Revolution 2 — Sound became synthesizable

Electronic oscillators allowed musicians to create sounds that did not previously exist acoustically.

---

## Revolution 3 — Recorded sound became playable

Sampling transformed recordings into instruments.

---

## Revolution 4 — Musical events became programmable

Sequencers, drum machines, and MIDI allowed musical behavior itself to become data.

---

## Revolution 5 — Sound became computational

DAWs, DSP, machine learning, and modern AI allow sound to be:

- analyzed
- transformed
- separated
- generated
- classified
- searched

algorithmically.

The underlying story of electronic music is therefore not merely a history of musical genres.

It is the history of humans gaining progressively finer control over **sound itself**.

First we learned to record it.

Then generate it.

Then edit it.

Then sequence it.

Then sample it.

Then manipulate it live.
